## Executive summary

The verified record supports the housewife role as a genuine, federally-scaffolded operations function: the USDA Cooperative Extension Service (Smith-Lever Act, 1914) built a nationwide delivery system — agents, group meetings, clinics, home visits, radio, TV, press — that taught meal preparation, clothing, home comfort, and housekeeping efficiency as systematized skills, with Depression-era curricula (nutrition, canning, gardening, poultry, home nursing, sewing) and WWI-era food-preservation/rationing programs as concrete precedents. That "housework as engineering" framing had already been formalized in 1919 by Christine Frederick's "Household Engineering," an explicit correspondence course applying Taylorist scientific-management principles to domestic tasks — decades before the 1950s era it shaped. But the popular image of a rigid, universal day-assigned schedule (Monday-wash-day-as-law, hour-by-hour charts) does not hold up: 1941 guidance literature explicitly treated Monday-only washing as an outdated point of pride and recommended flexible scheduling, and several specific claims about codified charts, ledgers, and time-motion figures failed verification outright. The honest weight of the labor is real: full-time housewives' hours stayed essentially flat (roughly 52 to 55 hours/week) from the 1920s to the mid-1960s despite new appliances, meaning technology shifted the composition of work, not its total volume — the invisibility, not the disappearance, of the labor is the documented finding. Finally, the canonical "How to Be a Good Wife" ten-step list has no traceable 1950s textbook origin at all; the "Housekeeping Monthly, 1955" page that circulates as its source is a proven fabrication built from a 1957 magazine cover, so it should not be used as evidence of period doctrine.

## Verified findings

### [high] The USDA Cooperative Extension Service — established by the 1914 Smith-Lever Act with the mission to 'help people help themselves' — was the institutional backbone delivering home-economics guidance nationally. Home demonstration agents worked with farm, community, and urban families, delivering it through group meetings, clinics, office and home visits, exhibits, radio, television, and the press. The homemaking content itself covered meal preparation, clothing selection, home comfort, and housekeeping efficiency, explicitly framed as bringing families 'the latest research and information to help them achieve better living.'

*Evidence:* Three claims from the same primary USDA/NAL source (Elsie Carper Collection) merged: one at 3-0, one at 2-1, one at 3-0. All corroborated independently across multiple state extension-service histories (NC, TX, AR, MT, MS) covering the 1950s-60s 'heyday' period. This is the clearest, best-evidenced institutional finding in the set — it establishes the housewife role had a real professional-development infrastructure behind it, not just informal custom.

- https://www.nal.usda.gov/collections/special-collections/elsie-carper-collection-extension-service-home-economics-and-4-h

### [high] That Extension infrastructure delivered concrete, systematized curricula well before the 1950s: during the Depression, agents formally taught farm women nutrition, surplus food canning, gardening, poultry production, home nursing, and sewing as survival skills; during WWI, canning/drying preservation and rationed use of staple commodities (wheat, sugar, meat) were promoted nationally through Extension programming as a patriotic 'Food Will Win the War' effort.

*Evidence:* Both claims sourced from official USDA/NIFA history, each at a 2-1 vote, corroborated by NAL's 'Canning Through the World Wars' exhibit, University of Minnesota Extension history, NC State special collections (1917 canning schools), and National Archives WWI food-conservation records. Establishes that 'systematized household task as teachable skill' predates the 1950s by 30-40 years and was federally coordinated, not ad hoc.

- https://www.nifa.usda.gov/about-nifa/what-we-do/extension/cooperative-extension-history

### [high] Christine Frederick's 'Household Engineering: Scientific Management in the Home' (1919, American School of Home Economics, Chicago) was structured as an explicit correspondence course applying efficiency-engineering and Taylorist scientific-management principles to everyday housekeeping tasks — direct primary evidence that housework was formally framed as an operations/engineering discipline with teachable systems, decades before the 1950s homemaker era it shaped.

*Evidence:* Two closely related claims merged (one 2-1, one 3-0), both verified against the same primary Wellcome Collection record plus independent corroboration from HathiTrust, Internet Archive, Library of Congress, and rare-book dealers. Note: more specific downstream claims about this book's CONTENTS — a literal hour-by-hour weekly schedule and a documented two-month dishwashing time-motion experiment with specific minute figures — were both refuted (0-3) in this same verification pass, so the book's existence and framing are solid but its specific internal prescriptions should not be cited as confirmed without direct primary-text confirmation.

- https://wellcomecollection.org/works/hrjy4kug

### [medium] The 'wash day Monday' convention was already being treated as obsolete, optional folklore rather than binding rule in mainstream guidance literature by 1941: the manual recommends Tuesday or any convenient day instead, since Sunday leaves a Monday backlog, and even suggests splitting washing across multiple days.

*Evidence:* 2-1 vote on primary source (America's Housekeeping Book, NY Herald Tribune, 1941), directly quoted. Important counterweight for mechanism design: do not encode a rigid Monday-Tuesday-Wednesday day-assignment schedule as 'the historical system' — the actual guidance literature of the era pushed back against that rigidity. Several related and more specific schedule/chart claims (Frederick's detailed schedule, the Herald Tribune's own chart-instruction method, 'Blue Monday' folklore, ironing-follows-washing chaining) were refuted (0-2, 0-3) and should not be treated as confirmed.

- https://ia802806.us.archive.org/24/items/americashousekee00newy/americashousekee00newy.pdf

### [medium] Full-time (non-labor-force) housewives spent essentially as much time on housework in the early 1970s as in the 1920s (roughly 52 hours/week in 1924 to 55 hours/week in 1966, a ~6% rise over four decades) despite five decades of household technology — appliances shifted the composition of tasks (less cooking, more shopping/childcare) without reducing total workload.

*Evidence:* Only a 1-1 vote survived (an incomplete/split verification, the weakest in the confirmed set), though the verifier noted strong corroboration from Joann Vanek's original time-budget studies and multiple peer-reviewed sources (NBER, Journal of Economic History, Social Forces). This is the load-bearing 'honest weight' finding — the labor didn't shrink, it went unmeasured and unpaid — but note a competing, refuted claim set (Ramey-Francis) argues the decline was real (just smaller than Lebergott's 42-hour claim, ~6 hours) and driven by fertility/education changes rather than a flat trend — the exact magnitude and shape of the trend is contested in the literature, not settled by this research pass.

- https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/time-spent-in-housework/

### [high] The famous ten-step 'How to Be a Good Wife' list has never been traced to any real 1950s home-economics textbook, and the circulating 'Housekeeping Monthly, 13 May 1955' magazine-page image is a proven fabrication — a mock-up assembled after the text list had already circulated by e-mail, built by pasting that text around an image lifted from a 1957 John Bull magazine cover (a watermark on the circulated image is dated 1990, and no magazine called Housekeeping Monthly is registered in any US/UK archive).

*Evidence:* Two claims merged, both 3-0 on the same secondary (but rigorous) fact-check source, cross-corroborated by Wikipedia, Intellectual Takeout, Dusty Old Thing, and primary archival holdings of the actual 1957 John Bull issue. Practically important: this artifact is the single most-cited 'primary document' of 1950s domestic doctrine in pop culture, and it should be explicitly excluded from any Maude design rationale as a real historical text — cite it only as a documented modern fabrication if referenced at all.

- https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/how-to-be-a-good-wife/

## Caveats (verbatim from the verification pass)

Several "confirmed" claims carried split or thin votes even after adversarial review (0: 2-1, 2: 2-1, 4: 2-1, 6: 2-1, 7: 2-1, 8: 1-1 — an incomplete/split vote, the weakest in the surviving set), so treat those as well-sourced but not unanimous. More importantly for mechanism design: the REFUTED list is where the concrete, software-translatable artifacts lived, and none of them survived verification — Frederick's supposed hour-by-hour weekly schedule, her dishwashing time-motion figures, the Herald Tribune's chart-based day-assignment instructions, the budget-envelope/ledger distribution system, and the Home-Ec "objective rules" codification were all voted down (0-2 or 0-3). Do not port these into the Maude design as verified history — they may still be real (weak sourcing ≠ false), but this pass could not confirm them. The Ramey-Francis vs. Vanek vs. Lebergott dispute over whether housework hours fell 6 hours, 42 hours, or stayed flat 1900–1965 is a genuinely unsettled area of cliometric history, not a clean consensus — claim 8 leans Vanek's "essentially flat" reading but with a weak internal vote. Home Economics' academic-discipline origin story (Lake Placid Conferences, 1909 AHEA, "buymanship" curriculum) failed verification entirely and should be treated as an open gap, not an established fact. Sources are otherwise strong: USDA NAL/NIFA and Wellcome Collection are primary archival; Snopes is secondary but appropriate for a debunking claim. Nothing here is time-sensitive, but the hours-of-labor debate could shift with further economic-history work.

## Open questions

- What did the actual household budget/ledger/envelope systems look like in practice (format, distribution channel, adoption rate)? The claim about insurance-company/bank/extension-distributed record books and the '1/12 of annual fixed expenses' deposit method was refuted in verification (1-2) — this is a live gap given it's one of the most software-mechanism-relevant artifacts (a budgeting ledger) in the original question.
- Did Christine Frederick's or any contemporary's time-and-motion studies produce genuinely documented, reproducible task-timing data (e.g., her dishwashing experiment) that could be responsibly used as a historical baseline for task-duration modeling? This was refuted (0-3) and needs direct primary-text confirmation from the archive.org copy of Household Engineering rather than secondary description.
- Was there ever a widely-PRACTICED (not just recommended-against) day-assigned weekly chore schedule, and what's the earliest solid primary source for it, given that 'Blue Monday' folklore, the Herald Tribune's chart method, and Frederick's detailed schedule all failed verification in this pass?
- The original question asked about coupon clipping, trading-stamp economies (e.g., S&H Green Stamps), newspaper reuse, mending baskets, and pantry/canning inventory cycles specifically — none of these produced a surviving verified claim at all. This entire category of the original research question remains unanswered and would need a dedicated follow-up pass.
